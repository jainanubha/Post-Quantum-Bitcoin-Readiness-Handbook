# 13.2 Bitcoin’s collective cryptographic risk

## Individual security versus collective exposure

Suppose Alice migrates all her outputs to a correctly implemented PQ spending policy. Bob retains an exposed secp256k1 public key. Alice's direct signature-forgery risk may be reduced, but the value of her holdings depends on the same monetary network in which Bob participates. The BIP361 motivation is that widespread compromise of legacy authorization could damage confidence, liquidity and the incentives supporting network security, even for users who have already migrated.

This is an externality argument. It does not mean every vulnerable UTXO will be stolen or every theft will have the same market effect. A validly authorized transaction under existing consensus rules contains no reliable indication of whether its signature came from the original owner or someone who recovered a private key. Detection by chain analysis is not a consensus-level ownership test.

## A simple inventory model

At a fixed chain snapshot, let $U$ be the set of live UTXOs, $a_i$ the value of output $i$, and $e_i$ an indicator of a known exposed authorization path under a stated adversary model. Define

$$
V_{\mathrm{exposed}}=\sum_{i\in U}a_i e_i,
\qquad f_{\mathrm{value}}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{exposed}}}{\sum_{i\in U}a_i}.
$$

A count-based statistic uses $\sum e_i/|U|$ instead. These measure different things. Neither is a forecast of theft: an attacker may need additional secrets, script data, sufficient quantum resources and transaction inclusion. An unknown exposure classification should be kept separate from a verified negative. A full inventory would also record which spending paths are sufficient and whether keys have been disclosed outside the chain.

BIP361 cites a March 2026 exposure estimate in its motivation. This handbook does not adopt that number as an independently measured current statistic. A later research iteration should reproduce the methodology, specify block height, handle address/key reuse and report both output counts and value.

## Possible system-level consequences

| Mechanism | Potential effect | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large unauthorized transfers | Losses and market uncertainty | Attack capabilities, exposure inventory, market response |
| Competition over vulnerable funds | Fee competition or unusual transaction demand | Inclusion incentives, actual spending rules |
| Falling market confidence | Potential changes in mining revenue and security expenditure | BTC price, fees, subsidy and miner cost response |
| Deadline-driven demand | Congestion and unequal access to block space | Adoption curves and migration transaction mix |

A transfer of existing BTC changes control and possibly circulating supply; it does not mint additional BTC. The economic impact is conditional on how coins are spent and markets respond.

## Incentives and objections

The authors argue that a sunset aligns private incentives with a collective security goal. A critic may instead regard additional restrictions on old outputs as a loss of reliable access, especially if a rescue condition excludes valid historical wallet constructions. Both claims need to be assessed against alternative policies, not merely against a hypothetical perfect migration.
