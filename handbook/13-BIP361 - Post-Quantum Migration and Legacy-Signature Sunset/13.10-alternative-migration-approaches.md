# 13.10 Alternative migration approaches

## Comparison principles

Different proposals address different parts of the transition. Some create a secure destination; others change the conditions for using existing funds; others constrain the speed of spending or offer a recovery path. They should not be ranked using a single security label. Compare them under identical exposure and quantum-capability assumptions.

## Voluntary migration

An opt-in approach would make protected outputs available and allow holders to choose when to migrate. It minimizes immediate changes to legacy access but leaves residual exposed funds subject to existing rules. The success of the approach depends on availability of safe outputs, communication, economic incentives and whether holders act before attacks become feasible. It does not resolve the indistinguishability of a recovered scalar from a legitimate one.

## Scheduled restrictions and rescue

BIP361 proposes a known transition period followed by tighter legacy verification. This can reduce the potential attack surface if adoption and recovery work as intended. It can also leave legitimate holders without compatible authorization. Rescue is complementary to a restriction, not a guarantee of universal access. Its coverage and soundness must be assessed before evaluating the overall policy.

## Commit-then-reveal migration

Several developer discussions explore using an earlier commitment to intended migration so that the later revelation of a hidden key does not freely authorize a conflicting destination. The relevant security question is whether the commitment was binding, authorized and established before the adversary obtained the necessary information. Reorganization assumptions, censorship, commitment spam, expiration and fee flexibility matter. A design that only checks a prior hash without a robust authorization model could allow hostile commitments or denial of service. For already exposed keys, timing alone may not establish which party is legitimate. These are discussion proposals; their claimed benefits require independent evaluation.

## Hourglass-style restrictions

The Hourglass draft explores throttling spending from specified early public-key outputs. Its objective differs from owner authentication: a throughput constraint may limit the rate at which vulnerable funds move but cannot, on its own, establish who has the legitimate right to spend them. A restricted block budget may cause competition between genuine holders and attackers. BIP361's authors discuss compatibility with a separate Hourglass mechanism for P2PK if it has been activated by Phase B; the canonical draft does not itself activate Hourglass.

## Recovery proofs and hybrid transition

A future proof of independent knowledge could supplement legacy authorization. This depends on the availability and secrecy of that evidence and the proof's consensus safety. Hybrid spending policies may also help diversify authorization for new outputs, but an independently usable quantum-vulnerable branch can undermine protection. A hybrid policy does not retrofit existing UTXOs.

| Approach | Primary goal | Major unresolved issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary migration | Preserve choice and promote early adoption | Remaining vulnerable supply |
| BIP361 schedule | Coordinate transition and tighten legacy spends | Legitimate access and rescue coverage |
| Commit/reveal | Reduce some migration-time exposure races | Prior commitment security and inclusion |
| Hourglass | Limit rate of specified legacy spends | Does not distinguish owners from attackers |
| Independent-secret rescue | Restore access under stronger evidence | Coverage, proof security and verification cost |
