# 10.1 Introduction and problem formulation

Chapter 09 introduced the standardized post-quantum cryptographic primitives and their security assumptions. This chapter investigates a different problem: how to evaluate a digital signature scheme as a component of Bitcoin's transaction authorization system. A secure primitive is necessary, but is not sufficient, for a secure and practical consensus mechanism.

## Why Bitcoin is different

Bitcoin does not rely on a certificate authority to establish the identity of a spender. Full nodes independently determine whether a transaction satisfies the spending conditions of the referenced unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs). A signature authorizes a transaction according to the applicable signature hash and validation rules; it does not establish a person's identity or provide confidentiality. This environment imposes requirements that are not always decisive in other applications:

- **Replicated verification:** transaction validation consumes resources on many independent machines, not only the signing device.
- **Adversarial inputs:** an attacker can submit malformed signatures and attempt to exploit expensive validation paths.
- **Long-lived commitments:** coins may remain unspent for years, while the rules governing their spending conditions cannot simply be replaced by a wallet software update.
- **Scarce block space:** authorization data competes with other transactions for inclusion in blocks.
- **Decentralization and self-custody:** ordinary users must remain able to validate transactions and securely manage keys without relying on a privileged service.
- **Composition:** wallets and higher-layer protocols use properties beyond basic signature generation and verification.

## A conceptual design problem

Let $S_{pk}$ and $S_{\sigma}$ denote encoded public-key and signature sizes, $T_s$ and $T_v$ the costs of signing and verification, and $M_s$ and $M_v$ their peak working memory requirements. A candidate can be described by the resource vector

$$
\mathbf{c}=(S_{pk},S_{\sigma},T_s,T_v,M_s,M_v).
$$

This is not an established consensus cost function. Its components have different units and affect different participants. A smaller signature might require a more expensive verifier, or a faster signer might consume more memory. A meaningful comparison must also specify a threat model, parameter set, encoding, spending construction and device environment.

## Scope and learning objectives

This chapter develops evaluation criteria, compares representative signature families, examines Script constraints and considers multiparty signing, state management, hybrid policies and wallet requirements. It does not rederive the algorithms in Chapter 09, endorse a consensus change, or present unmeasured performance as fact. Detailed transaction serialization, fee and block-space calculations belong to Chapter 11; migration policy and P2MR are treated in later chapters. By the end, readers should be able to distinguish cryptographic readiness from deployability, identify evidence needed for candidate assessment, and explain why different Bitcoin use cases may favor different trade-offs.

## Discussion question

Suppose two schemes satisfy the same stated threat model. One has a smaller signature but costs substantially more to verify. Which additional information would be required before recommending either for a Bitcoin spending mechanism?
