# 10.7 Hash-based signatures for Bitcoin

Hash-based signatures are attractive for Bitcoin because they offer a family of constructions based on hash-function security rather than lattice or elliptic-curve assumptions. Chapter 09 introduces the primitives; here we examine the operational constraints of using them for transaction authorization.

## One-time and stateful signing

Lamport and WOTS+ signing keys have one-time usage requirements. Merkle-tree constructions such as XMSS and LMS authenticate many one-time keys under a common public key, but the signer must avoid reusing a leaf. NIST SP 800-208 specifies particular stateful schemes and operational requirements; a hypothetical Bitcoin wallet is not necessarily compliant with those requirements.

A stateless hash-based scheme such as SLH-DSA does not require a mutable signing counter, but its standardized signatures are substantially larger. Limited-signature variants may reduce overhead for applications that can bound the number of signatures. NIST's SP 800-230 is an initial public draft as of November 2026, not a finalized extension to FIPS 205.

## Why one UTXO does not mean one signature

A valid chain can consume an outpoint only once, but a wallet may sign many distinct messages authorizing alternative spends of that outpoint before confirmation. Signing happens off-chain; a rejected, abandoned or never-broadcast transaction may still have disclosed a signature to another party.

| Wallet event | Operational risk |
| --- | --- |
| Fee bumping / replacement | A different transaction digest needs a new signature |
| Restoring an old backup | Previously consumed signing state may be restored |
| Multiple devices | Independent signers may allocate the same one-time key |
| Pre-signed recovery transactions | Several alternative messages may be signed in advance |
| Reused keys | One signing key may authorize more than one UTXO |
| Lightning commitment updates | Signing frequency can be much higher than on-chain spending frequency |

A safe stateful wallet would need a durable allocation policy: reserve signing state before releasing a signature, prevent rollback, coordinate concurrent signers, and define recovery behavior when state is uncertain. Blockchain history alone cannot reconstruct every off-chain signing event.

## Bitcoin-oriented research constructions

**SHRINCS** combines an efficient stateful signing path with a stateless recovery option. Its original discussion describes a compact 324-byte stateful signature under specified conditions; the recovery path is larger. Static backups are possible because a device that has lost reliable state can use the stateless path instead of risking state reuse. Exact sizes and assumptions should be taken from the particular revision under review.

**SHRIMPS** explores compact hash-based signatures for multiple independent stateful signing devices. Its initial discussion describes signatures of roughly 2.5 KB under a limited-use configuration. Device and signing-volume assumptions are essential to understanding this result. Both constructions are research proposals, not deployed Bitcoin consensus mechanisms or final NIST standards.

These proposals motivate examining normal-case and recovery-case costs separately. A rarely used fallback may be expensive, but its feasibility during mass recovery or migration must still be considered. Privacy also matters: a revealed signing index or authentication-path structure may leak operational information depending on the construction.

## Research questions

What signing-volume bound is realistic for a single-use payment wallet, a cold-storage vault and a Lightning channel? Can a wallet maintain signing state across crashes and concurrent devices without a trusted coordinator? What evidence is needed to ensure that a stateless recovery path cannot be exploited as a downgrade or that its fees remain affordable?
