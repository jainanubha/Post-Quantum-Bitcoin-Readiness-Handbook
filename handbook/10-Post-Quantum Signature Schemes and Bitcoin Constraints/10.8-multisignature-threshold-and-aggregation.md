# 10.8 Multisignature, threshold signatures and aggregation

Bitcoin users often distribute authorization across devices, organizations or recovery participants. Replacing individual signatures is not enough to preserve these capabilities. This section separates several concepts that are frequently grouped together under the term multisignature.

## Authorization and compression mechanisms

| Construction | What it achieves | What it does not imply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Script-based multisignature | A spending condition requires independent signatures from an authorized set | A single compact group signature |
| Joint multisignature | Multiple signers jointly produce a signature under an aggregate key | An arbitrary threshold of participants |
| Threshold signature | A qualified subset of shares signs under a group key | Support for noninteractive aggregation of unrelated signatures |
| Aggregate signature | Multiple signatures or authorization statements are compressed into a shared representation | A threshold key-generation protocol |
| Batch verification | Multiple verification equations are checked together more efficiently | Reduced on-chain signature bytes |
| Cross-input signature aggregation | Authorization data across transaction inputs is combined under a specified construction | Existing consensus support or compatibility with all sighash modes |

MuSig2, specified by BIP327, is a Schnorr-based multisignature protocol for joint signing by all participating keys. FROST is a distinct threshold Schnorr signing protocol (RFC 9591). Neither automatically works when the underlying signature is ML-DSA, Falcon or SLH-DSA. Their constructions, nonce handling and security arguments depend on their respective algebraic setting.

## Independent-signature versus threshold spending

Consider a hypothetical 2-of-3 policy using a new verifier for ML-DSA-44. If the spending witness contains two independent raw signatures, they occupy $2\times2420=4840$ bytes before keys, policy data, lengths or other serialization. This example assumes the necessary consensus rules exist; it is not a valid current Bitcoin spending mechanism.

A threshold construction might instead produce one signature under a group public key. Its actual size, security and compatibility depend on the particular threshold protocol.

## Evaluating multiparty PQ protocols

A future comparative study should record the number of rounds, per-party computation and memory, total communication, key-generation and share-refresh mechanisms, tolerance of malicious participants, robustness against aborts, concurrency assumptions, signing randomness and resulting verification format. The model should distinguish a trusted dealer from distributed key generation and specify whether a coordinator learns sensitive material. A signing protocol can provide threshold unforgeability but still be impractical for offline or high-latency devices. Similarly, a compact aggregate may require expensive verification or an extra proof system with additional assumptions. Cross-input aggregation must bind all relevant inputs, keys and signature messages and account for participants who join or leave a collaborative transaction.

## Bitcoin privacy considerations

A group signature under a single public key can conceal details of the signing policy compared with revealing independent signatures in a script. However, privacy depends on the complete output and spending construction, not merely the signature count. Recovery branches, transaction patterns and coordination metadata can reveal other information.

## Research questions

Can a PQ threshold protocol provide a compact, independently verifiable output with practical communication and secure distributed key generation? Can aggregation lower block-space usage without making validation prohibitively expensive? Which benefits of Taproot and Schnorr can be retained, and which require new spending policies or protocol constructions?
