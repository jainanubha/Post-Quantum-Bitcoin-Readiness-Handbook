# 11.1 Introduction and problem formulation

Bitcoin's post-quantum readiness depends on more than the existence of quantum-resistant digital signatures. A replacement authorization mechanism must also fit into the network's finite validation, storage and communication capacity. A signature that is cryptographically attractive may require substantially more transaction data than the 64-byte Schnorr signature used by Taproot.

Chapter 09 introduced standardized post-quantum primitives. Chapter 10 examined their suitability for Bitcoin, including verification costs and consensus integration. This chapter isolates the data and economic dimensions. It asks how much block weight a concrete spending construction would consume, how that translates into a fee at a specified fee rate, and what this means for migration at scale.

## Learning objectives

After completing this chapter, readers should be able to:

- Calculate stripped size, total size, weight and virtual size from a transaction's serialized fields.
- Distinguish the length of a signature from the cost of a complete spending transaction.
- Construct a reproducible hypothetical model for PQ transaction authorization.
- Compare fees across input counts, signature formats and fee rates.
- Estimate a weight-constrained block capacity and explain why it is not an observed throughput forecast.
- Separate one-time migration expenditure from the recurring cost of spending PQ-protected outputs.

## Analytical framework

For a transaction design $d$, let $B(d)$ denote its stripped size, $D(d)$ the additional witness serialization bytes, and $r$ the chosen fee rate in sat/vB. The weight and size-based target fee are

$$
W(d)=4B(d)+D(d),\qquad v(d)=\left\lceil\frac{W(d)}4\right\rceil,\qquad F_r(d)=\lceil r\,v(d)\rceil.
$$

These quantities describe size and a chosen fee target, not the security or validity of the design. Consensus constraints, public-key exposure, signing-state requirements and verification resources must be considered separately.

## Scope and conventions

All examples use bytes (B), weight units (WU), virtual bytes (vB), and satoshis (sat). The fee rates are illustrative scenarios, not current fee estimates. PQ examples use standardized raw key and signature lengths from FIPS 204 and 205, plus separately labelled Falcon candidate lengths. They assume a hypothetical witness encoding; no such transaction is claimed to be accepted by current Bitcoin consensus rules. No equivalent security level is implied by a comparison with classical signatures.

The following sections first establish the existing accounting rules, then apply a controlled model, before considering capacity and migration. The estimator is specified on paper.
