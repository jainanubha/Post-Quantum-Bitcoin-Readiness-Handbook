# 11.9 Block-space and throughput implications

A transaction's weight consumes a portion of the network's finite block-space budget. Under the current BIP141 consensus limit, block weight cannot exceed 4,000,000 WU. For a homogeneous workload, an illustrative capacity can be calculated by dividing the ordinary-transaction budget by the weight per transaction.

## Simplified capacity model

Let $L=4{,}000{,}000$ WU and reserve $R=40{,}000$ WU for the header, transaction count, coinbase, other overhead and an illustrative margin. The reserve is a modelling assumption, not a protocol requirement. With ordinary-transaction weight $W$,

$$
N(W)=\left\lfloor\frac{L-R}{W}\right\rfloor.
$$

This is an upper-bound packing scenario for a fixed homogeneous workload, subject only to the assumed weight budget. It ignores verification cost, sigop limits, transaction dependencies, relay policy, miner selection, demand and other constraints. It does not count the coinbase as an ordinary transaction.

| One-input, two-output template | Weight (WU) | Tx per modelled block | Tx/s at 600 s/block |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P2TR key path | 616 | 6,428 | 10.71 |
| ML-DSA-44 | 4,289 | 923 | 1.54 |
| ML-DSA-65 | 5,818 | 680 | 1.13 |
| ML-DSA-87 | 7,776 | 509 | 0.85 |
| SLH-DSA-128s | 8,443 | 469 | 0.78 |
| SLH-DSA-128f | 17,675 | 224 | 0.37 |
| Falcon-512 padded candidate | 2,120 | 1,867 | 3.11 |

The 600-second interval is a long-run target average, not a deterministic schedule. Transactions per second do not equal payments per second because one transaction can pay many recipients. The figures cannot be interpreted as observed throughput or expected future usage. The PQ rows may not be valid under existing rules, and no miner is assumed to accept them today.

## Mixed transaction workloads

A transition is unlikely to replace every classical signature simultaneously. Let $f$ denote the fraction of ordinary transactions by count using a hypothetical PQ format, and let $W_C,W_P$ be their respective weights. A mean-weight approximation is

$$
\bar W=(1-f)W_C+fW_P,\qquad N\approx\left\lfloor\frac{L-R}{\bar W}\right\rfloor.
$$

The result is a continuous-mixture approximation; an actual block must contain integer numbers of each template. For one-input, two-output transactions, using ML-DSA-44 as the M1 PQ format:

| PQ transaction share by count | Mean weight (WU) | Approx. ordinary tx/block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0% | 616.00 | 6,428 |
| 10% | 983.30 | 4,027 |
| 25% | 1,534.25 | 2,581 |
| 50% | 2,452.50 | 1,614 |
| 75% | 3,370.75 | 1,174 |
| 100% | 4,289.00 | 923 |

A minority of larger transactions can consume a substantial share of weight. A fraction of transactions by count is not the same as a fraction of weight, inputs, value or economic activity.

## Beyond weight

Witness bytes still contribute to block transmission and archival storage. Large witnesses may increase propagation and initial block download bandwidth requirements even when discounted in weight. Validation CPU and memory may become separate bottlenecks. Large public keys committed to directly in outputs could also increase UTXO storage, while keys revealed only at spending have a different footprint. A full evaluation must consider each resource separately.

Existing Bitcoin Core policy also restricts the weight of a standard transaction (400,000 WU in the linked policy source). That is distinct from the consensus block limit and may change with policy or future upgrades. A weight-only block scenario is not an assertion of relayability.
