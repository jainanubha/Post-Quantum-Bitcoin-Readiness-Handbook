# 9.6 FIPS 203: ML-KEM

ML-KEM is a module-lattice-based key-encapsulation mechanism derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber. Its purpose is to establish shared secret material, not to sign a message. The security of the construction is related to Module-LWE.

## Working principle

The recipient first generates an encapsulation key $ek$ and a private decapsulation key $dk$. The sender uses $ek$ to produce a ciphertext $c$ and a shared secret $K$. The recipient applies decapsulation to $c$ with $dk$ to recover $K'$. For a correctly generated ciphertext, the parties obtain matching secrets except with a very small correctness-failure probability.

$$
(ek,dk)\leftarrow\operatorname{KeyGen}(),\quad (K,c)\leftarrow\operatorname{Encaps}(ek).
$$

$$
K'\leftarrow\operatorname{Decaps}(dk,c).
$$

The underlying public-key encryption component (K-PKE) uses noisy polynomial relations to hide secret information. The KEM adds hashing, key derivation and a ciphertext consistency check in a Fujisaki–Okamoto-style transformation. An invalid but correctly sized ciphertext that fails the internal consistency check leads to implicit rejection: the algorithm derives fallback secret material rather than exposing a distinguishable validity result through its normal output. Input checks and implementation behavior still matter.

## Standardized parameter sets

| Parameter set | Category | Encapsulation key (B) | Decapsulation key (B) | Ciphertext (B) | Shared secret (B) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| ML-KEM-512 | 1 | 800 | 1,632 | 768 | 32 |
| ML-KEM-768 | 3 | 1,184 | 2,400 | 1,088 | 32 |
| ML-KEM-1024 | 5 | 1,568 | 3,168 | 1,568 | 32 |

Source: FIPS 203, section 8, tables 2–3. These are encoded sizes, not network packet sizes. The parameter names do not state the key length in bits.

## Security and Bitcoin relevance

Chosen-ciphertext security, secure randomness, side-channel protection and correct integration into an authenticated protocol are important for deployment. For Bitcoin, a useful case study is BIP324, the encrypted peer-to-peer transport protocol. Post-quantum key establishment could be investigated as a future transport design choice; ML-KEM is not part of BIP324 as specified. Similar questions arise for communication between wallets, hardware signers and custody services. A KEM does not protect existing UTXOs from forged ECDSA or Schnorr signatures. Replacing the transport key exchange alone would not change transaction authorization.
