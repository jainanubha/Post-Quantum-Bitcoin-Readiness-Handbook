# 9.1 Introduction

Bitcoin relies on cryptographic mechanisms to enforce spending conditions without requiring a central authority. Digital signatures allow validating nodes to check that a transaction satisfies the authorization conditions associated with an unspent transaction output (UTXO). Hash functions serve other purposes such as commitments, transaction identifiers and proof of work. Quantum computing affects these mechanisms differently. A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could solve the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem underlying Bitcoin's ECDSA and BIP340 Schnorr signatures. This is a change in the computational assumptions underlying these schemes, not simply a reduction in their classical security margin. Increasing the elliptic-curve key size alone would therefore not constitute a long-term post-quantum solution. Quantum search algorithms also affect hash-based constructions, but their consequences depend on the particular security property, output length and attack model.

## What is post-quantum cryptography?

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) develops cryptographic mechanisms intended to withstand known attacks by both classical and quantum computers. These algorithms run on conventional computers and they do not require quantum communication or quantum hardware. The term does not imply unconditional security: the confidence in a scheme depends on mathematical assumptions, cryptanalysis, parameter choices and implementation quality.

For Bitcoin, readiness involves more than discovering a replacement for elliptic-curve signatures. A migration would require agreement on verification rules, safe spending constructions, wallet support and a practical means of moving existing funds. Long-lived outputs make the timing of migration particularly important. A security improvement that becomes available only after an attack is feasible may be difficult to deploy safely.

## Why NIST standards matter

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has coordinated an open process for evaluating quantum-resistant public-key algorithms. Its standards provide stable algorithm definitions, parameter sets and encodings, making them a useful starting point for interoperable implementations and comparative research. This chapter studies those standards as cryptographic primitives, rather than as proposed Bitcoin consensus rules. We will distinguish key establishment from signatures and evaluate the evidence needed to move from a standardized primitive to a usable Bitcoin spending mechanism. Detailed signature comparisons, transaction-weight calculations and migration proposals belong to later chapters.
