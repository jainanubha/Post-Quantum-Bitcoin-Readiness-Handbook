# 12.7 Security and quantum threat analysis

## Threat model

Consider an adversary who can inspect confirmed transactions, public mempool data and other disclosed wallet information. A sufficiently capable cryptographically relevant quantum computer may recover a secp256k1 private scalar from a known public key. Hash commitments have different security assumptions and quantum search properties. The timing and cost of practical attacks are uncertain.

## Long-exposure risk

A P2TR output publishes its elliptic-curve output key throughout the lifetime of the UTXO. P2MR instead publishes a script-tree hash commitment. When the leaf scripts and keys remain unknown, the commitment removes that particular persistent public-key target. It does not establish that all information needed for an attack is secret.

For example, suppose a wallet exposes an extended public key or a descriptor containing a leaf key. A party who can derive or recognize the relevant public key may attempt key recovery independently of the output commitment. Whether recovered key material is sufficient to spend depends on the rest of the leaf policy and the ability to reconstruct the script proof. Key reuse across outputs can similarly expose keys through earlier transactions.

## Short-exposure risk

A classical `OP_CHECKSIG` leaf reveals its public key and signature when the witness is broadcast. A sufficiently fast quantum attacker may obtain the signing key and construct a conflicting spend, subject to the policy, access to proof data and transaction-confirmation dynamics. P2MR does not remove this exposure merely by hiding the leaf until it is used. The time window depends on network propagation, inclusion, replacement and confirmations.

| Scenario | Relevant protection or limitation |
|---|---|
| Fresh hidden leaf using classical signature | Removes output-key exposure; short-exposure risk remains |
| Leaf key already public or reused | Long-exposure risk may persist for that leaf |
| Leaf requires two independent classical keys | Attack depends on satisfying both and other conditions |
| Enforced leaf with a future secure PQ verifier | Could address signature-related quantum risks, subject to integration and assumptions |
| Unconditional or prematurely used upgrade leaf | May permit spending without the intended authorization |

## Hash security and the commitment

Under idealized classical models, a 256-bit hash offers approximately $2^{256}$ preimage-search work and $2^{128}$ generic collision-search work. Grover search yields an idealized quadratic improvement for unstructured preimages, while generic quantum collision algorithms have different time and memory trade-offs. These are asymptotic models, not concrete estimates of a quantum attack on a Bitcoin output. Replacing a chosen commitment, finding a collision, and recovering a low-entropy hidden script are distinct problems.

The root authenticates a particular tree only under the relevant commitment-security assumptions. An observer with a short list of possible scripts may test guesses much more cheaply than brute-forcing an arbitrary 256-bit preimage. Hiding a script is not encryption. The protection also depends on avoiding any independently usable weaker leaf and on correct wallet and node implementations.

## Security boundaries

P2MR does not change proof-of-work, protect previously exposed outputs retroactively, establish safe migration rules for legacy UTXOs or select post-quantum signature standards. It provides an output-level mitigation that can be evaluated independently of those concerns.

## Open questions

- What information is exposed by public descriptors, watch-only services and multiparty signing sessions?
- How should multi-target quantum risk be modelled across reused leaf keys?
- What security properties should a future PQ leaf satisfy beyond unforgeability?
