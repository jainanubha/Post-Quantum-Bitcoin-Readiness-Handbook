# 12.1 Introduction and motivation

BIP360 draft v0.12.1, pinned revision [e7263a4c](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/e7263a4cfe500c89e4269889244606953691ca33/bip-0360.mediawiki).

## Learning objectives

- Explain why a hash commitment and an elliptic-curve public key present different quantum attack surfaces.
- Identify the problem BIP360 addresses and the problems it deliberately leaves outside its scope.
- Understand the distinction between a proposal, its activation and its safe use by wallets.

## From cryptographic risk to output design

Chapters 03–08 establish that Bitcoin uses elliptic-curve signatures to authorize spending and that different output types reveal different information before spending. Chapters 09–11 examine alternative cryptographic primitives, validation requirements and economic costs. BIP360 brings these questions together at the level of an output construction.

A Taproot output commits to an x-only secp256k1 output public key. Even when the owner intends to spend exclusively through a script path, the output itself remains a public elliptic-curve point. Under a sufficiently capable quantum attack on discrete logarithms, recovering a corresponding scalar would enable key-path authorization without satisfying the intended script. The use of a NUMS internal key does not remove this risk: the attacker targets the final output point rather than the internal key.

P2MR takes a different approach. Its output commitment is a 32-byte Merkle root of a script tree, not a tweaked public key. The proposed validation mechanism requires a leaf and a path demonstrating membership in that tree. There is no separately authorized elliptic-curve key path. The change therefore addresses the persistent public-key exposure inherent in Taproot outputs while retaining selective revelation of scripts.

## What the proposal does and does not establish

| Question | Answer under draft BIP360 |
|---|---|
| Does the output publish an elliptic-curve authorization key? | No; it publishes a hash commitment. |
| Does it remove the Taproot-style key path? | Yes. |
| Can scripts still contain elliptic-curve keys? | Yes. |
| Does it add a post-quantum signature algorithm? | No. |
| Does it protect every existing Bitcoin output? | No; existing outputs keep their original rules. |
| Is it activated? | No; the reviewed specification is a draft soft-fork proposal. |

The intended protection is conditional. If a key used in a leaf becomes known independently, a quantum attacker may target that key even before the intended spend. If the key is revealed only when spending, sufficiently fast quantum recovery remains a concern. Moreover, every spendable branch must be secure; a strong recovery leaf does not compensate for a weaker unrestricted alternative.

## Why an incremental change matters

Choosing a standardized PQ signature and defining its Bitcoin consensus behavior are distinct engineering tasks. A script-tree output can be studied independently of any final signature selection. This separation potentially permits incremental improvements, but it also creates dependencies: adding an output type alone cannot resolve all signature, migration or wallet problems.

The chapter treats P2MR as a research subject, not an instruction to send funds to witness version 2. Under the current rules an unrecognized witness version does not enforce BIP360's proposed authorization rules. Address encodability is not evidence of activation.
